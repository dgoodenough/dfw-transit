Board packet · Review of the v1 plan

Five riders read the DFW plan

Two commuters, a transit researcher, a software engineer and the federal transportation secretary's chair each read the 536-station, 44-line DFW Transit plan. Every number they cite comes from re-running the plan through a rebuilt model.

The panelists are fictional composites written by Claude. The federal seat speaks for the office of the U.S. Secretary of Transportation, not for any real person. The rebuilt model is described at the bottom of the page.

1 · What the project shows

HO
Okafor

This is a phased network, so it can be judged as one. Today about 5% of residents in the nine counties live within walking distance of a rail station, and transit is competitive with driving for about 96,000 daily commutes. The full plan raises those to 22% and 343,000. The average resident could reach three times as many jobs in 45 minutes. That's a real change for a region this size.

The shape of the curve matters more than where it ends. Most of the gain comes between 2035 and 2050. After 2050 the plan spends about $54B to add about 60,000 competitive commutes.

PR
Raman

It also shows good engineering instincts. One set of tables drives the map, the trip planner and the demand scores. When I loaded those tables cleanly, though, the station opening years and the track opening years disagreed on 238 of 642 line-to-stop links. The map, the planner and the scores were each drawing a slightly different network. The idea is right. The implementation had drifted.

MV
Vega

It shows my neighborhood. The Blue Line runs down East Lancaster through Poly, and the Main Street spine goes right past JPS. Nobody has ever drawn me on a rail map before.

US
The Secretary

It shows a region that already has the hard parts: freight corridors, two transit agencies running rail, and a new regional line in DART's Silver Line. What it lacks is a sequence that a funding partner can say yes to, one project at a time.

2 · What excites them

KT
Tran

The time slider. My manager thinks transit here is a fantasy. Watching the network grow out of something small does more than any argument I could make.

HO
Okafor

The cheap corridors are the most efficient spending in the plan. Forest Lane BRT returns about 80 competitive commutes per million dollars and Ross Avenue BRT about 66. The best metro line, Fort Worth's short Purple branch to TCU, returns about 15 and most metro lines return 1 to 4. Forest Lane is a 16 km BRT on an arterial that already exists. A plan that leads with corridors like that is one I would fund.

I also like that it reuses freight right-of-way and keeps the river crossings few. Those are the right constraints.

MV
Vega

The trip planner. I tried Poly to JPS for 2040 and got one transfer at Central Station. That's a real commute I could make.

PR
Raman

Plain CSV as the interface. It diffs in git, a non-programmer can edit it, and it doesn't lock anyone into a GIS license. Keep that.

US
The Secretary

A demand score for every station, published openly. Capital Investment Grant ratings rest on mobility, cost-effectiveness and land use. A plan that shows its own math is easier for a federal partner to say yes to.

3 · Where it misses

MV
Vega

Nothing changes for me until 2035. The first ten years of the slider are empty. When the trains do come, the model assumes they run often, but today's TRE and TEXRail come every 30 minutes or worse. If I miss one after a night shift, I wait half an hour in the dark. Frequency matters more to me than where the line is drawn.

KT
Tran

Collin County gets left behind. It has over a million people and the fastest growth in the region, yet only 10% of us live near a station even in 2070. Dallas and Tarrant counties reach about 30%. The biggest commute flows the old model found were McKinney and Frisco to Legacy, and the plan has no line that makes that trip.

Then the details. The McKinney commuter line opens in 2040 but connects to nothing until 2050. A stop on the Legacy Line is named "Eating Recovery Center Dallas / Plano", and there are three of them in a row. I wouldn't know where to get off.

HO
Okafor

Three problems with the method. First, cost. The v1 numbers used about $10M per km for commuter rail on freight right-of-way, but TEXRail came in near $24M/km and DART's Silver Line near $45M/km. The same per-line math also charged shared track two or three times. At realistic costs, the outer commuter legs (Bowie, Gainesville, Ennis) move from the best money in the plan to the worst.

Second, mode. Assign every competitive commute to its fastest path and the busiest proposed link carries roughly 1,000 people in the peak hour in the peak direction. That is a quarter of what a good BRT carries and about 5% of metro capacity. On commute demand alone, nothing here needs metro. Metro may still be right for speed, reliability and land use, but each metro line should have to beat a light-rail or BRT version of itself. The 93 km Arlington–Mansfield Purple line, at $12.6B and 0.75 commutes per $1M, is where to start.

Third, the plan isn't always connected. In 2045, 18 stations can't be reached from the rest of the system. The Midlothian line ends at Oak Cliff, about a kilometer short of the Red Line at Westmoreland, and is effectively stranded for 25 years. More than 50 pairs of stations share a name and a neighborhood, like the two Handleys, Uptown or Frisco, but sit 400 to 1,200 m apart with no transfer between them.

PR
Raman

The pipeline only runs on one Windows machine, and some inputs still come from a Downloads folder. Speeds were copied by hand into three places: the demand engine, the trip planner and the map. They had already disagreed once. There are no tests, no CI, and there's no way to compare two phasing plans except by eye. Rebuilding the tables also overwrites any hand edits. "Edit the CSV" is the promise, and regeneration quietly breaks it.

Line names need fixing too. Two lines are both called "Silver", and the map toggles them together. Meanwhile DART's real Silver Line, which opened in 2025, is labeled "Cotton Belt". Other lines are just named "3", "46" and "BRT".

US
The Secretary

The plan opens with Fort Worth's $6B downtown metro, which is its most expensive commitment. A federal partner would want the opposite: early, fundable projects that show ridership and build trust, then the big tunnel once the region has a delivery record. I'd also want to see the people the plan serves, not only the commutes. Car ownership, income and safety data aren't in the model yet. Without them nobody can say whether the 2035 network reaches the residents who need it most.

How the numbers were made

  • The v1 tables were converted, without changing any lines or years, into a normalized network and re-run through a rebuilt Python model with one shared configuration file.
  • Demand: 2020 census population and 2022 LODES jobs for 4,276 block groups in nine counties. The raw LODES origin-destination file is not in the repository, so commute flows come from a gravity model fitted to a 17 km mean commute (3.32M commutes in total). Trust the comparisons more than the absolute counts.
  • Travel time: walk up to 1.2 km or park-and-ride up to 8 km to commuter stations and line ends; wait half the headway, capped at 12 minutes; mode speeds based on stop spacing; 3-minute transfer penalty. Driving uses peak speeds (38 km/h, 1.35 detour factor, 8 minutes at the ends).
  • Costs: per route-km by mode and grade (BRT $15M, commuter rail $30M, surface metro $120M, tunnel $450M), plus stations. Shared track is charged once.
  • Today's quick-win test uses the 2030 network with the stated headways and the four BRT corridors opened early.
